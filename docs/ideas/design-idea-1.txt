Project brief
Working title: Workflow MRI
Subtitle: Turn chaotic business inputs into a governed operating system.

One-line pitch:
Workflow MRI ingests messy operational artifacts — spreadsheets, PDFs, emails, screenshots, notes — then maps the workflow, exposes bottlenecks and risks, proposes automations, and exports a cleaner operating model with auditability built in.

Why it matters:
Most companies already have the raw material for better operations, but it is trapped across files and inboxes. This project shows that you can not only extract structure from chaos, but also design the controls, review points, and measurement layer needed to make it production-worthy. That maps well to the broader shift toward AI systems that are tool-using, workflow-native, and dependable rather than just conversational.

Target audience:

Hiring managers looking for AI software engineers.

Founders or operators who want workflow automation.

Enterprise/internal-tools teams that care about governance and auditability.

What makes it stand out:

It is not a toy chatbot.

It demonstrates systems thinking, data modeling, process design, and AI governance.

It produces tangible business artifacts, not just summaries.

Core problem
The project should frame the pain clearly:

A fake company has process knowledge scattered across:

Spreadsheets with inconsistent columns.

PDF SOPs and forms.

Email threads with approvals and exceptions.

Screenshots of dashboards or tickets.

Notes from employees and managers.

The result is duplicated work, unclear ownership, missed handoffs, compliance risk, and no reliable way to estimate performance. Workflow MRI acts like an operations diagnostician: it converts fragments into a coherent map of how work actually happens.

Product outcomes
The app should generate these deliverables from one upload session:

Live operations dashboard.

Process map.

Cleaned data model.

Risk and privacy findings.

Human review queues.

Automation candidates.

Before/after time savings estimate.

Handoff package with tests, docs, and audit logs.

The key is that each output supports a different stakeholder: operators, managers, engineers, and compliance-minded reviewers. That makes the demo feel real and commercially relevant.

Scope and architecture
A strong version of the product can be organized into five layers:

Ingestion layer. Upload files or seed a sample company with fake artifacts.

Extraction layer. Detect entities, dates, owners, steps, exceptions, and approvals.

Process layer. Build a workflow graph and identify bottlenecks, loops, and duplicate work.

Governance layer. Flag privacy issues, uncertain extractions, and anything needing human review.

Output layer. Generate dashboards, exports, recommendations, and an audit trail.

This gives the project enough depth to feel serious without becoming too large to finish.

Demo flow
Here is the recommended demo flow, optimized for impact.

1) Landing view
Show a polished homepage with a single prompt:
“Upload your chaotic operations folder.”

The user sees sample inputs:

onboarding folder.

vendor management folder.

claims folder.

customer support folder.

Keep the language concrete so visitors instantly understand the use case.

2) Upload and ingest
The user uploads a mix of:

CSV/XLSX spreadsheets.

PDF docs.

email exports.

screenshots.

notes.

The app immediately displays:

file count,

detected document types,

extraction confidence,

items needing review.

This first step should feel magical but believable.

3) Workflow reconstruction
The app reconstructs the process as a graph:

intake,

triage,

review,

approval,

execution,

exception handling,

closure.

Display:

step sequence,

handoff owners,

cycle time,

repeated loops,

missing fields,

duplicate handoffs.

This is the heart of the “MRI” metaphor: it shows the hidden structure underneath the mess.

4) Risk and quality scan
Next, show a diagnostics panel with findings such as:

PII in free-text fields.

approvals missing evidence.

inconsistent status labels.

ambiguous ownership.

tasks that bypass review.

files with low-confidence extraction.

This is where the project becomes more than workflow mapping. It becomes a governed system.

5) Automation recommendations
Now show prioritized automation opportunities:

auto-route incoming requests,

prefill structured forms,

detect duplicate cases,

create SLA alerts,

generate summary notes,

route exceptions to human review.

Each recommendation should include:

estimated time saved,

confidence score,

required human oversight,

implementation complexity.

That makes the output feel actionable rather than generic.

6) Before/after simulation
Show a simple comparison:

before: fragmented process, unclear ownership, long turnaround times.

after: structured intake, explicit handoffs, review queue, audit trail, reduced cycle time.

A chart or metric panel works well here. Even a fake but plausible estimate is enough if it is clearly labeled as simulated.

7) Exportable handoff
End with an export screen:

process map PDF.

data schema JSON.

review queue CSV.

audit log.

test cases.

implementation backlog.

This final step makes the project feel like a handoff to engineering, not just an analysis dashboard.

UX details
To make the demo memorable:

Use a dark, enterprise-style UI with crisp status chips.

Make the process map interactive and visually dominant.

Include confidence indicators and “needs review” states.

Keep one sample company preloaded so the demo always works.

Add a “regen with safer mode” toggle to show governance awareness.

The goal is to show that you can build a product that is both impressive and trustworthy.
